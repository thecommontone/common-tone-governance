## Concepts Demonstrated

### 1. The Charity Super-Single → [[01.1 Pop Music, huh?]]

"We Are the World" was written specifically as a charity fundraising vehicle for African famine relief, recorded in a single overnight session (January 28, 1985) by 45+ of the biggest American pop stars of the era (Michael Jackson, Lionel Richie, Stevie Wonder, Bruce Springsteen, Tina Turner, Bob Dylan, Ray Charles, Diana Ross, and many others). The song became the fastest-selling American pop single in history, sold over 20 million copies, and raised approximately $63 million for famine relief.

The charity super-single is a specific commercial-cultural category. It followed the British template established by Band Aid's "Do They Know It's Christmas?" (December 1984) and preceded numerous subsequent charity super-singles: "That's What Friends Are For" (AIDS relief, 1985), "Hands Across America" (1986), "Voices That Care" (1991), the 2010 Haitian earthquake remake of "We Are the World" itself. Understanding the category illuminates how popular music periodically activates its commercial infrastructure for political / humanitarian purposes.

### 2. All-Star Collective Performance Structure → [[02.1 Texture]]

The record's structure rotates solo vocal lines across many different star performers: Lionel Richie opens, Stevie Wonder follows, Paul Simon takes a line, Tina Turner and Kenny Loggins trade lines, Ray Charles takes a phrase, Michael Jackson handles the chorus bridge. Each performer's solo moment allows their distinctive voice to be heard, and the full collective joins for the final chorus.

This rotating-soloist structure is specifically designed for multi-star collaboration. It allows maximum participation without any single performer dominating, gives listeners the pleasure of recognizing each voice, and creates a sense of collective effort that reinforces the song's charity purpose. Understanding this structural choice illuminates how specific collaboration formats generate specific musical-political effects.

### 3. Quincy Jones's Production → [[02.1 Texture]]

The record was produced by Quincy Jones, who had also produced Michael Jackson's *Thriller* (1982) — the best-selling album in history. Jones's production style emphasizes vocal clarity (each soloist is distinctly audible in the mix), dynamic building (the arrangement grows from solo voice to massed choir), and emotional directness. The production had to accommodate 45+ different voices with different vocal qualities while maintaining sonic cohesion — a significant engineering and mixing challenge.

Jones's production across his career (Sinatra arrangements, Count Basie work, Michael Jackson's major albums, *The Color Purple* film score, much more) represents one of the major Black American producer careers in popular-music history. Teaching "We Are the World" in this production context illuminates Jones's position as a producer who could work across genres and across multi-star collaborations successfully.

### 4. Jackson-Richie Songwriting Collaboration → [[01.1 Pop Music, huh?]]

Michael Jackson and Lionel Richie co-wrote "We Are the World." Both were among the most commercially successful Black American pop artists of the early 1980s (Jackson's *Thriller* era, Richie's peak solo career and Commodores legacy). Their collaboration for the charity single was itself symbolic — two Black pop superstars working together for humanitarian purposes.

The Jackson-Richie collaboration worked in specific ways: Jackson contributed melodic hooks and specific phrases; Richie contributed the gospel-adjacent verse structure and emotional directness. Both were working songwriters with deep catalogs (Jackson's Jackson 5 through *Off the Wall* through *Thriller*; Richie's Commodores work through his solo work). Teaching the collaboration illuminates how major songwriters collaborate when stakes are high — specific division of creative labor matching each writer's strengths.

### 5. Diatonic Major-Key Anthem → [[02.3 Primary Chords]]

The harmony is straightforward diatonic E major — primary chords with occasional secondary dominants, simple voice leading, no chromatic complexity. The simplicity is deliberate: an anthem song that 45+ vocalists and countless choirs would need to sing cannot have complex chord changes that would trip up performers or confuse listeners. Simplicity enables universal participation.

This is the same principle governing "Happy Birthday," "Amazing Grace," and other community-singing anthems. When a song is designed for group performance at scale, harmonic complexity becomes an obstacle rather than a craft asset. "We Are the World" is engineered for the largest possible performance accessibility.

## Concepts Demonstrated

### 1. All-Female Band Commercial Success → [[01.1 Pop Music, huh?]]

The Bangles — Susanna Hoffs, Debbi Peterson, Vicki Peterson, Michael Steele — were one of the first all-female rock bands to achieve sustained mainstream commercial success in America. "Walk Like an Egyptian" reached #1 in December 1986 and became Billboard's #1 song of 1987, making The Bangles the first all-female band to top Billboard's year-end chart. Their success opened commercial space for subsequent all-female bands (The Go-Go's had preceded them; Wilson Phillips, TLC, Destiny's Child, Spice Girls would follow).

Teaching The Bangles' commercial success illuminates how specific demographic demarcations shape popular-music history. All-female bands were commercially rare through the 1970s despite numerous individual female performers; the mid-1980s breakthrough of The Go-Go's and The Bangles represented a specific industry shift. Understanding this shift illuminates why the category mattered and why its emergence at that specific moment reflected broader cultural conditions.

### 2. Phrygian Mode Inflection → [[02.5 Modes]]

The song uses Phrygian-mode inflections (lowered 2nd scale degree) to evoke a "Middle Eastern" / "Egyptian" atmospheric setting. The characteristic move is the melodic F natural appearing over an E tonal center, creating the Phrygian ♭2 color that Western listeners code as exotic-Eastern. This modal coloring gives the song its distinctive atmospheric character.

The use of Phrygian (or Phrygian-dominant) mode to signify Middle Eastern / Egyptian / Arabic settings is a recurring Western popular-music convention. It appears in film scores (every Hollywood film set in the Middle East), some rock records (Dick Dale's surf-rock adaptations of Greek/Middle Eastern folk music), and in various pop references. This convention is related to the orientalist tradition we've tracked in earlier entries (Chinatown My Chinatown, Dardanella, Japanese Sandman, Witch Doctor). Phrygian = generic "Middle Eastern exotic" is a specific Western musical shorthand worth examining critically.

### 3. The Dance-Craze Novelty Song → [[01.1 Pop Music, huh?]]

"Walk Like an Egyptian" is partly a novelty dance song — the lyric instructs listeners to do a specific arm-position dance ("walk the way you walk"), and the song's commercial success included generating a minor dance craze. The 1986 Saturday Night Live era coincided with the record's peak popularity; Bangles music-video imagery showed everyone from cops to businesspeople doing the Egyptian-walk move.

Dance-craze novelty songs have a long lineage: "The Twist" (1960), "The Loco-Motion" (1962), "Macarena" (1996), "Harlem Shake" (2013), "Gangnam Style" (2012). Each provides a simple dance move that listeners can execute, generating viral participation that amplifies commercial success. "Walk Like an Egyptian" is a 1986 data point in this recurring tradition.

### 4. Verse-Chorus with Modal Harmony → [[04.6 Advanced Form]]

The song uses verse-chorus form with verses that stay in the Phrygian-colored minor mode and a chorus that brightens to a more major-sounding tonality. The modal shift matches the lyric structure: verses describe various people in various situations (all doing the Egyptian walk), the chorus delivers the universalizing thesis ("Walk like an Egyptian").

This modal-to-major chorus shift recurs in pop songwriting (compare "Happy Together" from the 1960s entry, "Copacabana" from the 1970s entry). The pattern provides emotional / harmonic contrast between more inward-facing verses and more outward-facing choruses. Teaching "Walk Like an Egyptian" in this tradition illuminates how modal-mixture structural techniques persist across decades.

### 5. Sternberg's Outside Songwriting → [[01.1 Pop Music, huh?]]

"Walk Like an Egyptian" was written by Liam Sternberg — a Cleveland-based songwriter who had offered the song to various female performers (reportedly Toni Basil rejected it) before The Bangles recorded it. Sternberg reportedly wrote the song after watching passengers on a ferry move awkwardly in stiff-armed positions during rough water.

The outside-songwriter-for-a-band pattern was increasingly unusual in 1986 rock (by this era, most successful bands wrote their own material). The Bangles themselves wrote other material on *Different Light*, but "Walk Like an Egyptian" came from outside. The song's success demonstrated that outside writers could still deliver hits to bands that otherwise self-composed, and that the Brill Building / commercial-craft songwriting tradition persisted despite the singer-songwriter-band dominance.
